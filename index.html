Project 01 · Regression

Estimating Property Prices from Structural & Area Characteristics

Method: Multiple Linear Regression
Tool: Excel (Data Analysis ToolPak)
n = 506 observations

Goal: estimate house prices using structural factors (room count, age) alongside area characteristics — crime rate, air quality, rainfall, and proximity to airports, waterbodies, and bus stops — to give developers and buyers a feature-level view of what drives value.

Fitted model Price = −17.26 − 0.21(crime_rate) − 6.99(air_quality) + 7.23(room_num) − 0.03(age) + 0.01(rainfall) + 1.19(airport) + 0.34(waterbody)
01 — FIT

R² = 0.55 — the model explains roughly 55% of the variation in house prices across the seven predictors tested.

02 — ROOMS

Room count is the single strongest lever: each additional room is associated with a meaningful price increase, holding other factors constant.

03 — CRIME

Areas with crime rates at or below 2.9 command consistently higher average prices than areas above 4.0.

04 — NOISE

Rainfall and waterbody proximity showed no statistically significant effect — price is driven by structure and safety, not by these environmental variables.

Recommendation. Developers see the highest return focusing on room count and low-crime locations; rainfall and waterbody access are not worth prioritizing on price grounds alone. Buyers should weight crime rate and air quality most heavily when assessing long-term investment value.

Project 02 · Classification

Loan Approval Prediction: Comparing Five Classifiers

Methods: Logistic Regression, KNN,
Decision Tree, Random Forest, MLP
n = 614 applicants · 70/30 split

Goal: determine which of five supervised models best predicts loan approval, then weigh the business cost of false approvals against false denials — the trade-off that actually matters to a lender.

Best model: Logistic Regression. Only 3 approved loans were wrongly denied (false negatives) out of 127 — the lowest of any model tested, ahead of Random Forest (4), KNN (6), Decision Tree (11), and the neural network (16).

Simplicity won here: with 614 records and near-linear decision boundaries, the smallest model generalized best.

01 — DRIVER

Credit history alone accounts for 34.2% of predictive importance in the Random Forest model — by far the dominant feature.

02 — COST

A false approval (~$10,000 lost principal) costs roughly 10× a false denial (~$1,000 in foregone interest) — so precision matters more than raw accuracy.

03 — COMPLIANCE

Logistic regression's coefficients are directly explainable — a requirement under ECOA/Fair Lending rules that black-box models like the neural network can't easily satisfy.

04 — THRESHOLD

Raising the decision threshold from 0.5 to ~0.7 trades a small amount of recall for meaningfully fewer false approvals — an estimated net benefit of ~$95,000 per 100 loans.

Ethical note. Gender and marital status were predictive but excluded from any deployment decision — using them would violate fair-lending law even where they improve accuracy. Credit history itself may encode historical inequity, which any production model needs a disparate-impact review to catch.

About

From raw table to recommendation

I work the way both projects above are written: start with the descriptive picture — distributions, pivots, the relationships that show up before any model runs — then bring in a predictive method suited to the question, and end on what a stakeholder should actually do with the result.

That means treating model choice as a business decision, not a leaderboard. In the loan project, the simplest model won because it was both more accurate and the only one a compliance team could explain. In the property project, the story was which variables didn't matter as much as which did.
